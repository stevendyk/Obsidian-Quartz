## The Theology of John Wesley: Practical Divinity

Wesley’s theology was intensely practical. He was an Anglican priest his entire life, but he blended various Christian traditions into a distinct theological framework that focused heavily on how God's grace transforms everyday life.

### 1. The Dynamic of Grace

Wesley broke down how God's grace works in a person's life into three distinct phases:

- **Prevenient Grace:** The grace that "goes before" us. Wesley believed God places a baseline level of grace in _all_ human beings, restoring enough free will for anyone to choose or reject God. It woos us before we even know it.

- **Justifying Grace:** The moment a person accepts Christ by faith, their sins are forgiven, and they are restored to a right relationship with God. This is an instantaneous shift in legal standing before God.

- **Sanctifying Grace:** The ongoing, lifelong process of being transformed by the Holy Spirit to become more like Christ. For Wesley, salvation wasn't just a ticket to heaven; it was the active healing of human nature.

### 2. Christian Perfection / Entire Sanctification

This is perhaps Wesley’s most unique—and controversial—contribution to theology. He argued that it is possible for a Christian to reach a state of "perfect love" in this life.

> **What it means:** A heart so entirely filled with love for God and neighbor that it no longer desires to willfully sin.
>
> **What it _doesn't_ mean:** Absolute perfection, infallibility, or freedom from mistakes, ignorance, or temptation. It is a maturity in love, not a flawlessness in execution.

### 3. The Wesley Quadrilateral

While Wesley never used this exact phrase, scholars later coined it to describe how he made theological decisions. He balanced four distinct sources to understand truth, with scripture always holding ultimate authority:

|**Authority Source**|**Wesley's Application**|
|---|---|
|**Scripture**|The foundational, primary authority for all Christian faith and practice.|
|**Tradition**|The historical witness and consensus of the early Church.|
|**Reason**|The essential human faculty used to process, interpret, and defend truth.|
|**Experience**|The personal, internal witness of the Holy Spirit confirming faith in the believer's life.|

### 4. Arminian Soteriology

Wesley was a staunch Arminian, putting him in direct theological conflict with his friend George Whitefield and the dominant Calvinism of his day. Wesley strongly rejected unconditional election (the idea that God chooses only certain individuals for salvation) and irresistible grace. Instead, he argued that Christ died for every single person, and that salvation can genuinely be accepted or rejected by human free will.

### Summary of Impact

John Wesley successfully married deep, personal piety with radical social action. He was a vocal opponent of the slave trade, an advocate for prison reform, and a pioneer in providing affordable healthcare and education to the poor. By the time of his death in 1791, his "methodical" approach to holiness had blossomed into a global movement that continues to shape denominations worldwide today, including Methodism, the Nazarene Church, and the broader Holiness and Pentecostal movements.

## [Social holiness: the communal pursuit of faith](https://www.logos.com/grow/wesleyans-methodist-beliefs/)

Wesleyans believe that holiness cannot be attained in solitude. John Wesley famously stated, “The gospel of Christ knows of no religion but social; no holiness but social holiness.”[4](https://www.logos.com/grow/wesleyans-methodist-beliefs/#easy-footnote-bottom-4-137258) In the Wesleyan tradition, “social holiness” refers specifically to the communal nature of the faith.

- **Societies:** Larger gatherings for preaching and worship
- **Classes:** Groups of about twelve people who met weekly for mutual support and spiritual accountability
- **Bands:** Smaller, intimate groups of three to five people where members transparently confessed their sins to one another and prayed for healing
